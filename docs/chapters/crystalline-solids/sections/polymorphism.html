Section 3.6 — Polymorphism & Allotropy
pp. 57–58

Most materials settle into a single crystal structure and stay there. A few do not. Polymorphism is the ability of a substance to adopt more than one crystal structure depending on temperature or pressure. When the material is a pure element the same phenomenon is called allotropy. The different structures are called polymorphs (or allotropes). Transitions between them are sharp, reversible, and governed by thermodynamics: the polymorph with the lowest free energy wins at a given T and P.

The practical payoff is enormous. Because the transition changes the unit cell — and therefore the density, hardness, magnetic character, and diffusion kinetics — engineers can choose a polymorph by controlling temperature. That is the foundation of every steel heat treatment ever devised.

The Sn transition is notorious in history. In the bitter Siberian winter of 1812–1813, French soldiers' tin buttons reportedly crumbled (though the retreat had many causes). In the 1850s Russian campaign accounts, tin military equipment developed gray powdery patches — the transformation was called tin pest or tin disease. Organ pipes in cold northern European churches suffered the same fate; restorers still find evidence of the disease in medieval pipes.

Carbon's polymorphism is pressure-driven rather than temperature-driven. At ambient conditions graphite is the stable form — soft, black, sp² sheets weakly stacked. Above roughly 3.5 GPa the same carbon atoms reconfigure into diamond, the hardest natural material, with every atom sp³-bonded in a tetrahedral network. At room temperature diamond is metastable (not converting back to graphite in any reasonable timeframe), which is why your engagement ring is safe.

Why it matters
Polymorphic transitions let engineers dial a material's properties — hardness, magnetic behaviour, carbon solubility — by controlling temperature and pressure alone, without changing composition. That is the atomic-scale logic behind every quench, anneal, and case-hardening recipe in metallurgy.

Heat steel, cool it, and you've rearranged its atoms — that's metallurgy.

Skipped: thermodynamics of the transition (Gibbs free energy)

Each polymorph has its own enthalpy $H$ and entropy $S$. The stable phase minimises $G = H - TS$. At the transition temperature $T_t$, two phases have equal $G$, so $\Delta G = 0$ and $T_t = \Delta H / \Delta S$. Above $T_t$ the higher-entropy (usually higher-symmetry or looser) phase wins; below it the lower-enthalpy (usually denser, more bonded) phase wins. Pressure enters via the $+PV$ term in $G$, favouring the denser polymorph at high $P$ — hence diamond over graphite.
