Every rule we derived for unit cells assumed a perfect, unbroken lattice. Real materials are almost never that clean. The same crystallographic ideas apply, but the scale and orientation of the crystal matter enormously — they determine whether a material feels the same from every direction or behaves very differently depending on which way you push it.

Grain boundary
A grain boundary is a ~2–5-atom-wide transition zone where two misoriented lattices meet. Atoms there are out of register — higher energy, faster diffusion, preferential corrosion sites. Finer grains mean more boundary area and generally stronger (harder-to-slip) material — this is the basis of Hall-Petch strengthening.

Anisotropy is the dependence of a property on direction. Because atoms are not equally spaced in all directions, the elastic modulus, thermal conductivity, optical index, and many other properties differ with crystallographic direction. Lower symmetry crystal systems are more anisotropic; among cubics, tungsten happens to be nearly isotropic while iron and copper show strong directionality.

The chart makes the pattern vivid. Aluminium is mildly anisotropic (63–76 GPa). Copper and iron are strongly anisotropic: iron's $[111]$ modulus is more than twice its $[100]$ value. Tungsten, despite being cubic, is elastically isotropic — all three bars are identical at 384.6 GPa. This is a rare coincidence of its elastic constants.

A isotropic polycrystal arises when grain orientations are random: the anisotropy of individual grains averages out and the bulk material responds the same from any direction. Most structural metals in service are in this state.

However, processing can impose a preferred orientation called crystallographic texture. The classic case: silicon–iron transformer cores are cold-rolled so that a $\langle 100 \rangle$ direction aligns with the magnetic flux path. Because $[100]$ is the easy magnetization direction in iron, this grain-oriented electrical steel has substantially lower hysteresis loss — saving significant energy at grid scale.

Texture — anisotropy by design
When engineers cold-roll electrical steel and align $\langle 100 \rangle \parallel$ magnetic field, they are deliberately engineering grain texture to exploit — rather than average away — crystal anisotropy.

Inside every spoon there are millions of tiny crystals — each with its own grain of opinion.
