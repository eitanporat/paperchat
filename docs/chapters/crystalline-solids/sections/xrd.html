We now know how to describe crystal structures on paper — unit cells, Miller indices, packing fractions. But how do experimentalists actually determine an unknown structure? The answer is X-ray diffraction (XRD). X-rays have wavelengths (0.05–0.25 nm) comparable to interatomic spacings, so a crystal acts as a three-dimensional diffraction grating.

The geometry is captured by Bragg's law. Consider two parallel atomic planes separated by interplanar spacing $d_{hkl}$. An X-ray beam strikes both planes at glancing angle $\theta$. The ray reflected from the lower plane travels an extra path of $2 d_{hkl} \sin\theta$ compared with the ray from the upper plane. Constructive interference — a peak — occurs only when that extra path is an integer number of wavelengths:

Move the slider to θ ≈ 30° (where 2d sinθ = 1.000d ≈ λ) to see the two reflected waves snap into phase and the sum double in amplitude — a Bragg peak. At θ ≈ 60° the path difference equals 1.73d; if λ = 1.73d that too would be a peak (second-order). At intermediate angles the waves partially cancel and no peak appears in the diffractogram.

Systematic absences — reflection rules

Not every $(hkl)$ family produces a peak. In BCC and FCC crystals, extra atoms inside the unit cell scatter rays that are exactly out of phase with the corner atoms for certain $(hkl)$, completely cancelling the reflection. The surviving peaks follow strict rules called selection rules (or reflection conditions).

The pattern of allowed peaks is a structural fingerprint: BCC gives a different sequence of $2\theta$ positions than FCC, letting you identify the lattice type directly from a diffractogram without any assumed model.

The X-ray diffractometer

A diffractometer has three key parts: a fixed X-ray tube (T), a specimen stage (S) that rotates by angle θ, and a detector (C) that always rotates by 2θ — keeping the detector on the reflected beam. The powder method ensures that among thousands of randomly oriented crystallites, some grains will satisfy Bragg's law for every plane family — so every allowed $(hkl)$ registers a peak as the detector sweeps through $2\theta$.

Press Sweep to watch the detector scan from 30° to 130°. Worked example — BCC Fe, (220) planes

Step by step

Given: BCC iron, $a = 0.2866$ nm, $\lambda = 0.1790$ nm (Co K$\alpha$), plane $(220)$, $n = 1$.

1. Check selection rule: $h+k+l = 2+2+0 = 4$ (even) ✓ — BCC allows this reflection.

2. Compute d: $d_{220} = \dfrac{0.2866}{\sqrt{4+4+0}} = \dfrac{0.2866}{2\sqrt{2}} = 0.1013$ nm.

3. Apply Bragg's law: $\sin\theta = \dfrac{n\lambda}{2d} = \dfrac{0.1790}{2 \times 0.1013} = 0.884$.

4. Solve for angle: $\theta = \arcsin(0.884) = 62.1°$, so $2\theta = 124.2°$.

Noncrystalline (amorphous) solids

The chapter closes by returning to amorphous solids. Crystalline and amorphous SiO₂ share the same local chemistry: every Si is tetrahedrally bonded to four O atoms. The difference is whether those tetrahedra are arranged in a periodic pattern (quartz crystal) or a random network (silica glass). Because XRD requires long-range periodicity to produce sharp peaks, an amorphous material gives only a broad hump, not a fingerprint pattern.

Which materials go amorphous? Most metals crystallize readily because their non-directional metallic bonds allow atoms to rearrange quickly. Most ceramics can be either crystalline or glassy depending on processing; complex silicates with many components are particularly prone to glass formation. Polymers are typically semi-crystalline — their long chain geometry prevents complete crystallization.

Why it matters
A diffraction pattern is a crystal's fingerprint; Bragg's law is how we read it. The sequence of peak positions identifies the lattice type; the spacings yield $a$; the intensities encode atomic positions — and the absence of sharp peaks reveals glass.
Skipped: structure factor and peak intensities

The selection rules above are a simplified consequence of the full structure factor $F_{hkl} = \sum_j f_j \exp[2\pi i(hx_j + ky_j + lz_j)]$, where $f_j$ is the atomic scattering factor and $x_j,y_j,z_j$ are fractional coordinates. A peak vanishes when $|F_{hkl}|^2 = 0$. For BCC the body-center atom at (½,½,½) produces a phase shift that cancels corner contributions whenever $h+k+l$ is odd. Peak intensities (as opposed to mere presence/absence) encode the positions of all atoms in the basis and are the foundation of full crystal-structure determination by Rietveld refinement.
