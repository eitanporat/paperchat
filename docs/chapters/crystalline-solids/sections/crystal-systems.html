Section 3.4 · Crystal Systems
pp. 59–60

Every crystal has a repeating building block — the unit cell. Six numbers fully describe its shape: three edge lengths $a$, $b$, $c$ and three interaxial angles $\alpha$ (between $b$–$c$), $\beta$ (between $a$–$c$), and $\gamma$ (between $a$–$b$). Together these are the lattice parameters.

When you ask which combinations of edge-length equalities and right-angle constraints are geometrically distinct, the answer is exactly seven. These are the seven crystal systems.

A common point of confusion: FCC and BCC are both cubic — they share the same unit cell shape ($a = b = c$, all angles 90°). What distinguishes them is where the atoms sit inside that box. This is why we need a second term:

Crystal system vs. crystal structure
The crystal system describes only the shape of the unit cell — the six lattice parameters and their relationships. The crystal structure also specifies the positions of atoms within that cell. FCC, BCC, and simple cubic are three different structures that all belong to the same cubic system. HCP is a different structure in the hexagonal system.

The seven systems range from maximally symmetric (cubic: three equal edges, all right angles) to minimally symmetric (triclinic: nothing equal, no right angles). Each additional constraint you relax opens a new class. Rhombohedral sits between cubic and triclinic — it keeps all edges equal but lets the angles deviate from 90°.

Seven shapes are enough to describe every crystal that has ever existed.

Skipped: 14 Bravais lattices Within each crystal system there are multiple ways to place lattice points — not just at corners but also at body centers, face centers, or base centers. This gives 14 distinct Bravais lattices (e.g., face-centred cubic and body-centred cubic are two Bravais lattices within the cubic system). Crystal structures are built by placing a basis (one or more atoms) at each Bravais lattice point.
