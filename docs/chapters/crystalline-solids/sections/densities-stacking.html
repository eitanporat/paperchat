Section 2.5–2.6 · Linear & Planar Density · Close-Packed Stacking
pp. 76–79

Unit cells describe how atoms pack in three dimensions, but two finer measures — linear density and planar density — tell us how tightly atoms line up along a specific direction or face. These numbers govern which planes slip past each other during plastic deformation and how fast atoms diffuse.

Linear density

To find the linear density along direction [uvw], draw the direction vector across the unit cell and count atom centers it passes through — a corner atom contributes ½ (only half its center lies on the vector segment inside the cell), while an atom whose center falls squarely on the interior of the segment contributes 1. Divide by the vector's length.

Planar density

Planar density works the same way on a 2D slice. For the FCC (110) plane: the rectangular face has four corner atoms each contributing ¼, and two edge-midpoint atoms each contributing ½, giving $\frac{4}{4}+\frac{2}{2}=2$ atoms. The rectangle's dimensions are $4R$ (along [001]) by $2R\sqrt{2}$ (along [1\overline{1}0]), so the area is $8R^2\sqrt{2}$.

Close-packed planes and stacking sequences

A close-packed plane is the most densely packed 2D arrangement of hard spheres. Between any two touching atoms lie triangular depressions — two interlocked sets. Label the first layer of atom centers A. The depressions directly above fall into two groups: B (triangle pointing up) and C (triangle pointing down). Only one type can be occupied at a time because B and C overlap.

Place the second layer in B sites. The third layer can go back to A (directly above the first layer) or into the previously unoccupied C positions. That single choice produces two distinct crystals with identical APF = 0.74.

The single choice that splits two crystal structures
FCC and HCP are the same close-packed plane stacked two different ways — that single choice flips ductility.
