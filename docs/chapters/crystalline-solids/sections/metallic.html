Most metals crystallise into one of three structures — FCC, BCC, or HCP. A fourth — SC (simple cubic) — appears in essentially one element. Understanding each structure means knowing (a) how many atoms actually belong to a unit cell, (b) how tightly the atoms are packed, and (c) the relationship between the cube edge length $a$ and atomic radius $R$.

How to count atoms per cell

A unit cell shares its boundary atoms with neighbouring cells. The rule is simple: count the fraction of each atom that lies inside the cell boundary.

Lattice parameter vs atomic radius

Atoms are assumed to be hard spheres. The lattice parameter $a$ is determined by which direction atoms actually touch along — and that direction differs between FCC and BCC.

The atomic packing factor (APF) tells us what fraction of the cell volume is filled by atoms.

For HCP the additional geometric parameter is the c/a ratio. The ideal value, derived by requiring equal touching in-plane and between layers, is $c/a = \sqrt{8/3} \approx 1.633$. Real metals deviate slightly: Zn has $c/a \approx 1.856$ (elongated), Be has $c/a \approx 1.568$ (compressed).

A unit cell is a recipe; the crystal is what you bake from it.
Specify $n$, $a$ (and $c/a$ for HCP), and you have determined the density, diffraction pattern, slip systems, and packing of an entire infinite lattice from a box containing just 1–6 atoms.
Skipped: coordination number derivations and HCP stacking sequence

The coordination number CN = 12 for FCC can be shown by noting each atom has 4 nearest neighbours in its own layer plus 4 above and 4 below. The FCC stacking sequence is ABCABC (three distinct layer positions), while HCP is ABABAB (two). Changing stacking sequence while keeping packing the same is how FCC and HCP are related by stacking faults.
