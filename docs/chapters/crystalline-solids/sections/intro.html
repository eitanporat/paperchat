Every solid material owes its properties — strength, color, transparency, electrical behavior — to how its atoms are arranged in space. The central distinction is between crystalline and amorphous solids.

In a crystalline solid, every atom sits at a predictable position — the pattern repeats in three dimensions over distances of millions of atoms. All metals are crystalline when solidified slowly, as are many ceramics and some polymers. In an amorphous solid, no such long-range rhythm exists. The atoms are frozen in place but in a disordered tangle, the way they might look in a snapshot of a liquid. Window glass is the archetypal example.

To reason about crystals precisely, we use three foundational ideas. The hard-sphere model treats each atom as a solid sphere; neighboring atoms in the crystal are in direct contact. The lattice is the abstract 3D grid of points — one per atom site — that captures the repeating geometry. The unit cell is the smallest repeating box: stack identical unit cells edge-to-edge in all three directions and you rebuild the whole crystal.

The difference between crystalline and amorphous is not just academic — it shows up in everyday properties. Crystalline ceramics and polymers tend to be opaque, while the amorphous form of the same material is often transparent. Magnesium (Mg) crystallizes in a hexagonal close-packed (HCP) arrangement: its crystals are opaque and cleave along specific planes. Silicon dioxide (SiO₂) can form either crystalline quartz (opaque, anisotropic) or window glass (amorphous, transparent, and the same in every direction). Same atoms, radically different arrangements, radically different behavior.

The core idea
A crystal is what you get when atoms agree on a rhythm and stick to it.
